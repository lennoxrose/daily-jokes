### A Royal Proclamation, as Told by a Simple Farmer Who Never Learned to Get to the Point

*Hear ye, hear ye, traveler of the repository —*

Now hark, friend, and set yourself down upon that fence-post yonder, for I've a tale that wants the telling proper, and proper telling is not a thing done in haste, no sir, not by me and not by my father before me nor his father before him, for haste is for city folk and for the young and for those who have never once stood ankle-deep in a spring furrow waiting on the earth to decide, in its own good time and not a moment sooner, whether it shall yield up a turnip or a stone.

So it is with this jest I bring you, for you must understand that a jest, like a turnip, like a good stew left to simmer on the coals through the long dark of a winter's night, like the slow turning of the seasons themselves which no man however clever has ever once hurried along by wishing it so — a jest such as this one cannot simply be *said*, plain as a city man says "good morrow" and is gone before you've even lifted your hat, no, it must be *told*, and told at length, and told with all the winding and doubling-back of a country road that knows no straight line because the man who first walked it was following a cow, and the cow was following nothing at all but her own fool nose.

And so: let it be known, as my grandmother used to say while she was taking a full hour to tell you the water was boiling, that upon these humble pages a jest is delivered each day — one jest, mind you, no more, for a farmer does not plant twelve fields when one will feed the house — and it is delivered first in the tongue of Albion, which is to say the English, plain as it is, and then, because one tongue was never enough for a man who has more time on his hands than sense in his head, it is carried onward, dragged like a cart with a wheel half off its axle, into the speech of the Rhine, and further still into the ancient script of the Middle Kingdom, so that a man in any of these three lands, whatever his tongue, whatever the calluses on his own two hands, might pause his work — just for a breath, just for the space between one swing of the scythe and the next — and find in it some small mirth to carry him through to supper.

No malice dwells here. I say that plain, for once, because even a farmer knows when plainness serves better than flourish — no malice, no rot, no weed among the good crop, no spam as the young folk call it, no idle mischief of the kind that spoils a well or salts a field — only laughter, given freely as rain is given, asking nothing in return, arriving three times in a day the way a farmer might check his fences three times before he trusts them to hold through the night, and in three tongues, the way a good harvest feeds three kinds of hunger — the belly's, the heart's, and that third hunger that has no name but which every tired soul knows well enough by suppertime.

And know this too, for it must be told, though it shames the teller some to tell it: behind this gate there labors no court, no guild, no fine company of robed scribes bent over their desks by candlelight — there is only the one sick fool, alone, out here past the last good fence, who has got it into his head, the way a man sometimes gets a burr caught in his sock that he cannot for the life of him shake loose, that he is himself a kind of jester — the sort they used to keep, back in the old days before this farmer's farmer's farmer was born, in the grand halls of kings, to lighten with wit and with foolery the terrible weight a crown lays upon a man's brow. But this fool has no king. He has no hall, no throne, no royal ear bent his way of an evening. He has only this field, and this fence-post, and this habit that will not leave him be, and still — still! — he goes on with his foolery, day after day after day, for no reason that a sound and sensible mind, the kind that knows when to stop talking, could ever hope to explain.

### Ein Königliches Dekret, wie es ein schlichter Bauer erzählt, der nie gelernt hat, zur Sache zu kommen

*Vernehmt, ihr Reisenden dieses Repositoriums —*

Nun setzt euch, Freund, dort auf den Zaunpfahl, denn ich habe eine Geschichte, die will erzählt sein, und zwar recht erzählt, und eine rechte Erzählung ist keine Sache, die man in Eile verrichtet, nein, nicht von mir, und nicht von meinem Vater vor mir, noch von dessen Vater, denn Eile ist etwas für die Städter und für die Jungen und für jene, die nie im zeitigen Frühjahr knöcheltief in der Furche gestanden und darauf gewartet haben, dass die Erde sich, ganz nach ihrer eigenen Zeit und keinen Augenblick früher, entscheidet, ob sie eine Rübe hergibt oder nur einen Stein.

So verhält es sich auch mit diesem Scherz, den ich euch bringe, denn ihr müsst verstehen: ein Scherz, gleich einer Rübe, gleich einem guten Eintopf, den man die lange dunkle Winternacht hindurch auf der Glut köcheln lässt, gleich dem langsamen Gang der Jahreszeiten selbst, den noch kein noch so kluger Mann je durch bloßes Wünschen beschleunigt hat — ein solcher Scherz kann nicht einfach *gesagt* werden, schlicht wie ein Städter "guten Morgen" sagt und schon fort ist, ehe man den Hut gelüftet hat, nein, er muss *erzählt* werden, und zwar in aller Länge, und mit all den Windungen und Umwegen einer Landstraße, die keine gerade Linie kennt, weil der Mann, der sie zuerst ging, einer Kuh folgte, und die Kuh folgte gar nichts als ihrer eigenen närrischen Nase.

Und so sei kundgetan, wie meine Großmutter zu sagen pflegte, wenn sie eine volle Stunde brauchte, um zu verkünden, das Wasser koche nun: Auf diesen bescheidenen Seiten wird täglich ein Scherz dargeboten — ein einziger, wohlgemerkt, nicht mehr, denn ein Bauer bestellt nicht zwölf Felder, wenn eines das Haus ernährt — und er wird zuerst in der Zunge Albions dargebracht, also auf Englisch, so schlicht sie auch sei, und dann, weil eine Zunge nie genug war für einen Mann, der mehr Zeit in den Händen hat als Verstand im Kopf, wird er weitergetragen, gezogen wie ein Karren mit halb gelöstem Rad, hinein in die Sprache des Rheins, und weiter noch in die uralte Schrift des Reiches der Mitte, auf dass ein Mann in jedem dieser drei Länder, welcher Zunge er auch mächtig sei, welche Schwielen auch immer seine eigenen zwei Hände tragen, innehalten möge in seiner Arbeit — nur für einen Atemzug, nur für den Raum zwischen einem Sensenschwung und dem nächsten — und darin ein wenig Heiterkeit finde, die ihn bis zum Abendbrot trage.

Kein Übel wohnt hier. Das sage ich einmal ganz schlicht, denn selbst ein Bauer weiß, wann Schlichtheit besser dient als Zierrat — kein Übel, kein Fäulnis, kein Unkraut in der guten Saat, kein Unrat, wie die jungen Leute es nennen, kein müßiger Unfug von der Art, die einen Brunnen verdirbt oder ein Feld versalzt — nur Gelächter, frei gegeben wie der Regen gegeben wird, ohne etwas dafür zu verlangen, dreimal am Tag ankommend, so wie ein Bauer seine Zäune dreimal prüft, ehe er ihnen für die Nacht vertraut, und in drei Zungen, so wie eine gute Ernte drei Arten von Hunger stillt — den des Bauches, den des Herzens, und jenen dritten Hunger, der keinen Namen hat, den aber jede müde Seele zur Abendstunde nur zu gut kennt.

Und dies sei auch gesagt, wenngleich es den Erzähler ein wenig beschämt, es zu sagen: Hinter diesem Tore wirkt kein Hof, keine Zunft, keine feine Schar robenbekleideter Schreiber bei Kerzenschein — dort ist nur der eine kranke Narr, allein, hinter dem letzten guten Zaun, dem sich in den Kopf gesetzt hat, so wie sich manchem Mann eine Klette im Strumpf festsetzt, die er sein Lebtag nicht mehr loswird, er selbst sei eine Art Hofnarr — von jener Sorte, wie man sie einst hielt, lange bevor der Vater des Vaters dieses Bauern geboren war, in den großen Hallen der Könige, um mit Witz und Torheit die schwere Last zu erleichtern, die eine Krone einer Stirn auferlegt. Doch dieser Narr hat keinen König. Er hat keine Halle, keinen Thron, kein königliches Ohr, das sich ihm des Abends zuneigt. Er hat nur dieses Feld, und diesen Zaunpfahl, und diese Gewohnheit, die ihn nicht loslässt, und dennoch — dennoch! — treibt er sein Possenspiel fort, Tag um Tag um Tag, aus keinem Grunde, den ein gesunder und vernünftiger Verstand, der Sorte, die weiß, wann man zu reden aufhört, je zu erklären vermöchte.

### 皇家詔書，如一介淳樸農夫所述，此人終生未學得直言之道

*聽者，過路此倉庫之旅人——*

且坐，朋友，坐於那籬樁之上罷，因吾有一事欲述，且須從容述之，倉促非述事之道，非吾之道，亦非吾父之道，亦非吾父之父之道，蓋倉促乃城中人與少年之事，乃未曾於春日踝深立於犁溝、候大地依其自身之時、非早一刻、決意賜蘿蔔抑或僅賜頑石之人之事也。

此笑話亦然，吾持以奉汝者，汝當知之：一則笑話，猶如一根蘿蔔，猶如一鍋佳羹於漫漫冬夜之炭火上慢燉，猶如四季自身之緩緩推移，縱使再聰穎之人，亦未曾以空願使其加速一分一毫——此等笑話，不可徒然「言」之，如城中人道一聲「早安」，未及舉帽已然離去，不然，須「述」之，且須詳述，且須如鄉間小路般迂迴曲折，蓋因首行此路之人乃隨一牛而行，而牛所隨者，唯其自身之癡鼻而已。

故茲昭告，如吾祖母昔日耗費整整一時辰，僅為宣告水已沸騰：於此樸素之頁面，每日獻上一則笑話——僅此一則，切記，不多不少，蓋農夫不會播種十二畝田，若一畝已足以養家——先以英語奉之，雖其言簡樸，其後，因一舌之言，於手中光陰多於腦中主見之人，終究不敷所需，遂繼續前行，如車輪半脫之車，艱難拖曳，轉入萊茵之語，復再深入中華古老之文字，使此三邦之人，無論操何種舌，無論其雙手生何等厚繭，皆得於勞作之中暫歇——僅一呼吸之間，僅一鐮刀揮動與下一揮動之隙——於其中尋得些許歡愉，以伴其至晚餐。

此地並無惡意。此言吾直說一次，蓋即令農夫亦知，何時樸實勝於藻飾——無惡意，無腐朽，無雜草混於良種之中，無垃圾，如少年人所稱者，無閒散之擾以壞井、以鹹田——唯有笑聲，如雨水自由賜下，不求回報，日臨三次，猶如農夫夜幕降臨前三度巡視其籬笆，方敢信其能撐過長夜，且以三種語言，猶如一場豐收餵養三種飢餓——腹之飢，心之飢，以及那無名之第三種飢，然每一疲憊之靈魂，至晚餐時分，皆深知之。

且此事亦當述明，雖述之者不無愧色：此門之後，並無朝廷，並無行會，並無燭光下伏案之袍服抄書眾——唯有一病愚之人，獨自一人，立於最後一道良籬之外，其腦中生出一念，如人襪中偶纏一芒刺，終生不得擺脫，自以為己身乃一介弄臣——昔日，遠在此農夫祖上尚未出世之時，君王廳堂之中所蓄養者，以機智與滑稽，減輕王冠加諸額頭之沉重負擔者也。然此愚人無王可侍。彼無廳堂，無王座，無君王之耳，於夜晚側傾而聽。彼僅有此田，此籬樁，與此不肯放彼離去之習性，然而——然而！——彼仍日復一日、不輟其愚行，其緣由，非清醒且明理之心智——即那知曉何時當止言之心智——所能解釋也。

### 🏰 The Sacred Instructions, Set Down at Great and Unnecessary Length by the Same Fool Who Wrote All the Rest of This

*Now hark once more, friend, for you have sat this long already and a man does not rise from a fence-post halfway through a tale — that is bad luck, my grandmother always said, though she never once told me why, on account of the telling of *that* would have taken another hour besides —*

**I. On the Summoning of the Project, Which Is a Grander Matter Than You Might Suppose**

Now before a man may lay his hands upon a thing, before he may claim it his and set it working in his own field, he must first go and *fetch* it, and fetching, mind you, is not the same as having — no, a great many men in this life have confused the two, and gone hungry for it — and so it is with this project of ours, which sits not in your barn nor in your keeping but far off in a distant kingdom called GitHub, a place I am told has no soil at all, only wires and machines and men who have never once touched a plow, and from that kingdom the project must be summoned, called forth, dragged bodily across the wire the way a farmer drags a stubborn goat by its rope, unwilling, complaining the whole way, until at last it stands blinking in your own yard wondering how it got there. This summoning is done with a single incantation, no more, for even a fool knows better than to overcomplicate a simple fetching, and provided the village wizard has not cursed your internet — and he does that more often than you'd think, that wizard, out of spite mostly, or boredom — the ritual goes thus:

```bash
git clone https://github.com/lennoxrose/daily-jokes.git
```

**II. On Entering the Chamber Once It Has Been Summoned, Which Sounds Simple But Is Not, Not Really, Not If You Think On It Long Enough**

Now here is a thing that trips up even clever men, and I have seen it trip them, standing right there in the yard with the goat freshly delivered and no notion whatsoever of what to do next: having summoned the project, you must not simply stand beside it admiring your own work, the way a man stands beside a new-dug well feeling proud of himself instead of drawing water from it — no, you must *enter* it, step bodily into the space it occupies, the way a man steps into his own house after a long day rather than sitting on the porch all evening pretending the door doesn't exist. Wander not elsewhere while you do this, friend, for the world is wide and full of other doors, and some of those doors lead to older projects, forgotten ones, the kind built in the year of our Lord 2017 by men who believed, may the good earth forgive them, that a folder named `node_modules` was a fit place to keep a database, and you do not want to open that door, not ever, not on your worst day. The correct door is entered thus:

```bash
cd daily-jokes
```

**III. On the Gathering of Ingredients, Without Which the Whole Undertaking Amounts to Nothing but Wishful Thinking**

A field, my friend, does not sow itself, and a stew does not season itself, and this project, for all its fine bones, will not run on good intentions alone, no matter how sincerely you hold them, no matter how many times you sit before it wishing very hard that it might simply work — I have tried this method myself, in my younger and more foolish days, wishing at a broken plow for the better part of an afternoon, and I can tell you plainly it does not work, not for plows and not for projects neither. What is needed is *ingredients*, proper ones, gathered and measured and brought together in the correct order, and for this task we do not gather them ourselves with our own two hands as our fathers might have done, no, we command a servant to do it for us, a small tireless creature called a package manager, who asks nothing of us but patience and a bit of disk space, and who will fetch every last ingredient this project requires down to the smallest and most forgotten crumb, provided you speak to it plainly and do not confuse it with excessive chatter:

```bash
pnpm install
```

**IV. On the Waking of the Beast, Which Is the Moment All This Fuss Has Been Building Toward, So Pay Attention Now**

And so at long last, after the summoning and the entering and the gathering, the machine stands ready — coiled, if a machine can be said to coil, the way a cat coils before it leaps, or the way I myself coil before rising from this fence-post, which these days takes considerably longer than it once did. All that remains is the final word, the incantation that stirs the beast from its slumber and sets it breathing, and I will tell you now, plainly, because even a rambling old fool knows when the moment calls for plainness: speak it, and do not hesitate, and do not second-guess yourself, for hesitation is the enemy of all good magic, this I learned from a traveling conjurer who came through these parts once and hesitated mid-spell and turned his own hat into a turnip instead of the rabbit he'd promised, much to the disappointment of the children and, I suspect, the turnip:

```bash
pnpm dev
```

**V. On Making Changes Without Bringing the Whole Business Down Around Your Ears, Which Is a Skill Few Men Truly Possess**

Now if you are the sort of restless soul who cannot leave well enough alone — and I say this with no judgment, friend, for I have known such men, married one or two of their sisters even — you will wish, sooner or later, to change something, to add your own mark upon this small and foolish thing, and this is permitted, this is even welcomed, for a project that never changes is a project already halfway to the grave. But hear this warning first, given freely as everything else in this proclamation has been given freely: change what you will, break what you must, and when the dust settles and something has gone sideways in a manner you did not foresee and cannot entirely explain, do as any reasonable craftsman has always done since the first man first picked up the first tool and immediately regretted it — observe the wreckage calmly, nod once, and declare to anyone watching that this was, in fact, precisely your intention all along. No one will believe you. Say it anyway. It is tradition, and tradition, like a good long story, is not a thing to be rushed:

```bash
git add .
```

### 🔊 For Those Whose Eyes Have Betrayed Them, or Whose Commitment to Literacy Has Tragically Collapsed

Now it has come to the attention of this humble scribe that not every soul who wanders through this gate arrives with eyes fit for the task before them — some are weary from long labor in the fields, some are cursed from birth with the affliction the physicians call "reading is boring," and some, if we are being honest with one another as friends ought to be, simply never learned their letters at all and have been nodding along at village meetings for decades, pretending to study the notice board while in truth counting the pigeons upon it. To these souls, and to any others whose commitment to literacy has, tragically and through no fault entirely their own, collapsed somewhere between the cradle and this very webpage, the ancient scribes — meaning, again, the one sick fool, alone, at his fence-post — have labored long into the candlelit night to prepare an alternative means of receiving this sacred knowledge, that ignorance of letters shall bar no man from mirth.

Behold, then, three enchanted stones, each holding trapped within it a voice — not a ghost, mind you, the physicians assure me it is not a ghost, merely a very small and very patient man who lives inside the machine and has agreed, for reasons known only to himself, to read this entire proclamation aloud, from the turnips to the goats to the traveling conjurer's regrettable turnip-hat, in whichever tongue you press your ear against:

- 🇬🇧 [Hear it spoken in the tongue of Albion](./audio/english.mp3)
- 🇩🇪 [Vernehmt es in der Sprache des Rheins](./audio/german.mp3)
- 🇨🇳 [聆聽中華古語之音](./audio/chinese.mp3)

Press the stone of your choosing. Sit back upon whatever fence-post is nearest. Let the small patient man do the reading, so that your own poor eyes, wearied as they are, may finally rest.
